\section{Problem Statement}

This project addresses the need for accessible, individualized skill practice
that provides autistic learners with meaningful activities and gives caregivers
and educators interpretable information about their performance.

\subsection{Problem}

Autistic individuals differ in their strengths, communication styles, sensory
preferences, and support needs. These needs can also change over time.
Consequently, learning activities must accommodate individual differences rather
than assume that the same challenge, presentation, or assistance will suit every
learner. The World Health Organization identifies this diversity and the
importance of accessible support~\cite{whoAutism}.

For autistic learners seeking support with everyday routines, functional
communication, or remembering task information, useful practice must balance a
clear learning purpose with an understandable and engaging experience.
Caregivers and educators need to select relevant activities, adjust the
difficulty and presentation, and account for the learner's preferences. An
activity's educational purpose alone does not establish whether an individual
can comfortably participate in it or wishes to repeat it.

Caregivers and educators also need to understand what changes in activity
performance mean. A higher score may reflect greater familiarity with a task
or reduced difficulty.
Without information about these conditions, comparisons across sessions can be
misleading. Research on the
planning-assessment game ShopAutiPlan illustrates the value of examining how a
task is completed alongside whether it is completed. However, interpreting game
measures as evidence of a broader ability requires validation specific to the
activity and intended population~\cite{shamhan2026}.

The problem addressed by this project is therefore the need to bring together
individually appropriate practice, voluntary engagement, and contextualized
performance information within a manageable caregiver-supported experience. The
intended deliverable is a unified suite of learning games that allows caregivers
and educators to configure practice and review a learner's activity history.

The initial scope comprises three activities addressing routine sequencing,
functional communication, and short-term memory. These activities will provide
opportunities to practise selected skills and observe performance under
documented conditions. The project will not diagnose autism, assign an overall
developmental level, or treat improved game performance as proof of improvement
outside the activities.

\subsection{Stakeholders}

\textbf{Autistic learners} are the primary users and beneficiaries. Their
priorities include relevant activities, understandable instructions, comfortable
interaction, and the ability to communicate preferences and
stop participating.

\textbf{Caregivers and educators}, including parents, guardians, support
workers, and teachers, administer activities and interpret results. They need to
configure practice without extensive technical knowledge and understand
performance in the context of the activity and difficulty selected.

\textbf{Schools, learning centres, and support organizations} are potential
adopting organizations. Their interests include compatibility with available
equipment, manageable staff effort, accessibility, and appropriate handling of
learner information.

\textbf{Domain advisers}, including special-education professionals,
communication specialists, and autism researchers, may inform activity selection
and review the interpretation of results. Their role is advisory; no existing
partnership is assumed.

Learner perspectives will be considered alongside caregiver and professional
input. This approach reflects the importance of supported choice and
communication emphasized by the Autistic Self Advocacy
Network~\cite{asanChoice}.

\subsection{Environment}

The initial target population is autistic children aged six to 12
participating with a caregiver or educator. This boundary limits the content and
evaluation scope; age alone will not determine whether an activity is suitable
for a particular learner.

The activities will support learners who can make intentional selections among
visual choices through a supported input method, independently or with
assistance accessing the controls. Independent reading and spoken responses will
not be prerequisites. Caregivers may explain or demonstrate instructions.

Participation needs will be described explicitly rather than through a single
``high-functioning'' or ``low-functioning'' category. This recognizes that an
individual's abilities and support needs can differ across tasks and
settings~\cite{nasSupport}.

The intended settings are homes, schools, and learning centres. The initial
release will operate through a web browser on supported desktop, laptop, and
tablet configurations, with internet access available during sessions.
Interaction will accommodate keyboard, pointer, and touch input within the
declared supported configurations. Sessions will allow caregiver assistance,
breaks, and early stopping.

The initial scope excludes specialized input hardware, unsupervised multiplayer
interaction, clinical assessment, and replacement of an established
communication aid.

\section{Goals}
The following five goals define the project's core benefits and the evidence
needed to demonstrate their achievement. Success will be evaluated through
software verification, content review, and formative evaluation with intended
users. These evaluations will establish whether the prototype meets its project
goals; they will not establish clinical effectiveness.

\subsection{Personalize practice to individual learning needs}
\label{sec:personalize}

Enable caregivers and educators to select an appropriate difficulty level for
each learner and activity. Independent configuration across
activities will accommodate differences in a learner's strengths and support
needs.

\textbf{Success criterion:} Each of the three core activities provides at least
three meaningful challenge levels. At least 80\% of
participating caregivers or educators can configure an activity for a specified
learner scenario without developer assistance following a brief introduction.

\subsection{Provide purposeful, repeatable skill practice}

Provide three complete activities addressing routine sequencing, functional
communication, and short-term memory. Each activity will offer repeated practice
toward a clearly defined objective, with feedback that helps the learner
understand the task and their response.

\textbf{Success criterion:} All three activities support a complete practice
session and have a documented connection between their learning objective, task,
feedback, and performance measures. Each activity's content and interpretation
limits will be reviewed by a relevant domain adviser, with identified concerns
addressed before final evaluation.

\subsection{Offer an engaging experience that respects learner choice}

Provide activities that intended learners find enjoyable and are willing to
participate in, while preserving their ability to pause, decline, or stop.
Engagement will be evaluated for each activity rather than inferred from
completion scores or time spent playing.

\textbf{Success criterion:} For each core activity, at least 80\% of
participating learners who try it provide a positive enjoyment response through
an accessible feedback method. Evaluation will also report voluntary
continuation or replay choices, early stopping, and unavailable feedback so that
the result does not conceal unsuccessful experiences.

Learner feedback and caregiver observations will be reported separately.
Findings will be limited to the participants and conditions evaluated.

\subsection{Make changes in performance understandable}

Provide caregivers and educators with accurate activity histories that support
decisions about whether to repeat, simplify, or advance practice. Results will
identify the activity and difficulty associated with each session so that
changes can be interpreted in context.

\textbf{Success criterion:} Reported results match expected outcomes across all
defined verification cases. At least 80\% of participating caregivers or
educators correctly interpret at least 80\% of representative progress examples,
including examples involving changes in difficulty.

The product will report performance within defined activities rather than
combine unrelated results into an overall developmental score.

\subsection{Support accessible and comfortable participation}

Allow learners to use the core activities through supported interaction methods
and sensory settings. Accessibility will address how learners access an
activity, complementing the focus on learning challenge in
Section~\ref{sec:personalize}.

\textbf{Success criterion:} Every core activity can be completed through each
declared supported input method, with audio and nonessential motion disabled.
Learners can pause or end activities, and no essential task depends exclusively
on precise dragging, sound, or colour.

The interface will undergo review against applicable Web Content Accessibility
Guidelines criteria and cognitive-accessibility guidance, with the evaluated
scope and unresolved limitations documented. Familiar presentation and
configurable interfaces are supported by cognitive-accessibility guidance from
the World Wide Web Consortium~\cite{w3cPersonalization}.

\subsection*{Evaluation Basis}

The 80\% thresholds are proposed acceptance targets intended to require success
for a clear majority while exposing unsuccessful experiences. They are not
clinical benchmarks. Formative evaluation will aim to include at least five
caregivers or educators and at least five intended learners per activity;
participant counts and individual outcomes will be reported alongside
percentages.

Detailed procedures will be specified in the verification and validation plan.
Participation will depend on recruitment and applicable university requirements.
If intended learners cannot participate, expert review may support design
improvements, but the learner-engagement goal will remain unverified.

\section{Stretch Goals}

The following extensions will be considered after the core goals are met.
They expand presentation options and opportunities for shared play.

\subsection{Expand caregiver-controlled customization}

Allow caregivers and educators to further customize the visual
presentation of games to match individual learners' needs and
preferences. Options will include in-game brightness, colour palettes,
and the amount of decorative detail displayed. This extension will
provide more control than the basic accessibility settings included
in the core scope.

\textbf{Success criterion:} A caregiver or educator can adjust and save
these presentation preferences for an individual learner, and the
preferences are applied consistently across the core games. Supported
combinations preserve readable instructions and distinguishable
interactive elements.

\subsection{Enable multiplayer interaction}

Allow learners to participate in games with other players, creating
opportunities for shared play and interaction. Caregivers or educators
will be able to select multiplayer activities appropriate to the
learners' needs and preferences.

\section{Custom Documents (CDs)}

Two custom documents will record user needs and usability findings to support
project decisions and evaluation.

\subsection{Interview Report --- Fall Semester}

This report will summarize interviews with caregivers, educators, and,
where feasible, autistic individuals to identify learning needs,
accessibility preferences, and expectations for progress tracking.
The interview report will explain how the findings inform the project scope and
requirements, while acknowledging limitations in the perspectives
represented.

\subsection{Usability Evaluation Report --- Winter Semester}

This report will evaluate how intended users interact with the
prototype, including caregiver configuration, interpretation of
performance information, and learner participation in games.
The usability evaluation report will document usability findings, resulting
improvements, and
evaluation limitations, with results linked to the project goals.

\section*{Appendix --- Reflection}

The following responses describe what the team learned while defining the
problem, selecting goals, and setting the project scope.

\begin{enumerate}
    \item What went well while writing this deliverable?

    Writing the problem statement helped our team clarify the need behind
    our proposed suite of games. We examined why existing learning and
    recreational experiences may not meet every autistic learner's needs,
    what role our application could serve, and what we could reasonably
    claim about its impact.

    Discussing learner diversity helped us question the assumption that one
    difficulty level, interaction style, or sensory environment would suit
    everyone. We defined success around meaningful choices and adaptation
    to individual needs. Research and discussion also helped us distinguish
    game performance from broader developmental outcomes.

    Establishing measurable success criteria turned broad ideas such as
    ``accessible,'' ``personalized,'' and ``engaging'' into goals we can
    evaluate. This clarified what we need to demonstrate by the end of the
    project.

    \item What pain points did you experience during this deliverable, and how
    did you resolve them?

    Our largest challenge was balancing ambition with a scope we could
    implement and evaluate. Early discussions covered many developmental
    areas, game types, sensory settings, input methods, and analytics.
    We narrowed these possibilities to a core set of activities and moved
    additional features into stretch goals.

    We also had to decide what the application could responsibly claim.
    Terms such as ``improve,'' ``develop,'' and ``assess'' can suggest
    outcomes our software alone cannot establish. We therefore framed the
    project as providing practice opportunities and reporting performance
    within the activities.

    Defining engagement required more than completion scores or time spent
    playing. We chose to consider accessible learner feedback, voluntary
    continuation or replay, early stopping, and unavailable feedback.
    Caregiver observations will be reported separately so they do not
    substitute for learners' own perspectives.

    Finally, we needed measurable goals before evaluation procedures were
    fully defined. We set preliminary acceptance targets and documented
    their limitations, recognizing that the targets may need refinement
    during verification and validation planning.

    \item How did you and your team adjust the scope of your goals to ensure
    they are suitable for a Capstone project (not overly ambitious but also of
    appropriate complexity for a senior design project)?

    We narrowed the core scope to three activities addressing routine
    sequencing, functional communication, and short-term memory. These
    activities provide enough variation to investigate a shared platform
    while leaving time for accessibility, learner choice, configuration,
    and evaluation.

    We also defined personalization as caregiver and educator configuration
    rather than automatic decisions about what is best for a learner.
    This limits technical scope and avoids unsupported assumptions about
    individual needs.

    Our understanding of complexity shifted from the number of features to
    the care needed to support different users, maintain accessibility,
    record performance context, and make results understandable. We chose
    to prioritize depth and validation over a larger collection of games.

    Feedback from our supervisor, intended users, and Teaching Assistants
    (TAs) may lead to further scope changes. Our current goals leave room
    for that feedback while defining a substantial project we can evaluate.
\end{enumerate}
