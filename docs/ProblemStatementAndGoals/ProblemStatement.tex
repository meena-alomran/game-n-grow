\section{Goals}
The following five goals define the project's core benefits and the evidence
needed to demonstrate their achievement. Success will be evaluated through
software verification, content review, and formative evaluation with intended
users. These evaluations will establish whether the prototype meets its project
goals; they will not establish clinical effectiveness.

\subsection{Personalize practice to individual learning needs}

Enable caregivers and educators to select an appropriate challenge and amount of
assistance for each learner and activity. Independent configuration across
activities will accommodate differences in a learner's strengths and support
needs.

\textbf{Success criterion:} Each of the three core activities provides at least
three meaningful challenge levels and adjustable assistance. At least 80\% of
participating caregivers or educators can configure an activity for a specified
learner scenario without developer assistance following a brief introduction.

\subsection{Provide purposeful, repeatable skill practice}

Provide three complete activities addressing routine sequencing, functional
communication, and short-term memory. Each activity will offer repeated practice
toward a clearly defined objective, with feedback that helps the learner
understand the task and their response.

\textbf{Success criterion:} All three activities support a complete practice
session and have a documented connection between their learning objective, task,
feedback, and performance measures. Each activity's content and interpretation
limits will be reviewed by a relevant domain adviser, with identified concerns
addressed before final evaluation.


\subsection{Offer an engaging experience that respects learner choice}

Provide activities that intended learners find enjoyable and are willing to
participate in, while preserving their ability to pause, decline, or stop.
Engagement will be evaluated for each activity rather than inferred from
completion scores or time spent playing.

\textbf{Success criterion:} For each core activity, at least 80\% of
participating learners who try it provide a positive enjoyment response through
an accessible feedback method. Evaluation will also report voluntary
continuation or replay choices, early stopping, and unavailable feedback so that
the result does not conceal unsuccessful experiences.

Learner feedback and caregiver observations will be reported separately.
Findings will be limited to the participants and conditions evaluated.

\subsection{Make changes in performance understandable}

Provide caregivers and educators with accurate activity histories that support
decisions about whether to repeat, simplify, or advance practice. Results will
identify the challenge and assistance associated with each session so that
changes can be interpreted in context.

\textbf{Success criterion:} Reported results match expected outcomes across all
defined verification cases. At least 80\% of participating caregivers or
educators correctly interpret at least 80\% of representative progress examples,
including examples involving changes in difficulty or assistance.

The product will report performance within defined activities rather than
combine unrelated results into an overall developmental score.

\subsection{Support accessible and comfortable participation}

Allow learners to use the core activities through supported interaction methods
and sensory settings. Accessibility will address how learners access an
activity, complementing 2.1's focus on its learning challenge.

\textbf{Success criterion:} Every core activity can be completed through each
declared supported input method, with audio and nonessential motion disabled.
Learners can pause or end activities, and no essential task depends exclusively
on precise dragging, sound, or colour.

The interface will undergo review against applicable Web Content Accessibility
Guidelines criteria and cognitive-accessibility guidance, with the evaluated
scope and unresolved limitations documented. Familiar presentation and
configurable interfaces are supported by cognitive-accessibility guidance from
the World Wide Web Consortium~\cite{w3cPersonalization}.

\subsection*{Evaluation Basis}

The 80\% thresholds are proposed acceptance targets intended to require success
for a clear majority while exposing unsuccessful experiences. They are not
clinical benchmarks. Formative evaluation will aim to include at least five
caregivers or educators and at least five intended learners per activity;
participant counts and individual outcomes will be reported alongside
percentages.

Detailed procedures will be specified in the verification and validation plan.
Participation will depend on recruitment and applicable university requirements.
If intended learners cannot participate, expert review may support design
improvements, but the learner-engagement goal will remain unverified.

\section{Stretch Goals}
\subsection{Expand caregiver-controlled customization}

Allow caregivers and educators to further customize the visual
presentation of games to match individual learners' needs and
preferences. Options will include in-game brightness, colour palettes,
and the amount of decorative detail displayed. This extension will
provide more control than the basic accessibility settings included
in the core scope.

\textbf{Success criterion:} A caregiver or educator can adjust and save
these presentation preferences for an individual learner, and the
preferences are applied consistently across the core games. Supported
combinations preserve readable instructions and distinguishable
interactive elements.

\subsection{Enable multiplayer interaction}

Allow learners to participate in games with other players, creating
opportunities for shared play and interaction. Caregivers or educators
will be able to select multiplayer activities appropriate to the
learners' needs and preferences.

\textbf{Success criterion:} At least one game supports two or more
players in the same session, with each player's actions reflected in
the shared game state. Players can join, interact through the game's
supported actions, and leave the session. A supervising caregiver or
educator can manage participation and end the session.

\subsection{Expand the available practice areas}

Extend the suite with an additional activity addressing a learning
objective identified through stakeholder consultation. This extension
would broaden the available practice while preserving the consistency
of the learner and caregiver experience.

\textbf{Success criterion:} One additional activity satisfies the same
content-review, personalization, accessibility, and performance-reporting
criteria as the core activities. Its learning objective and interpretation
limits are documented, and its engagement results are evaluated
separately.

\subsection{Interview Report --- Fall Semester}

This report will summarize interviews with caregivers, educators, and,
where feasible, autistic individuals to identify learning needs,
accessibility preferences, and expectations for progress tracking.
It will explain how the findings inform the project scope and
requirements, while acknowledging limitations in the perspectives
represented.

\subsection{Usability Evaluation Report --- Winter Semester}

This report will evaluate how intended users interact with the
prototype, including caregiver configuration, interpretation of
performance information, and learner participation in games.
It will document usability findings, resulting improvements, and
evaluation limitations, with results linked to the project goals.

\begin{enumerate}
    \item What went well while writing this deliverable? 
    One of the most valuable parts of developing this deliverable was that
    it forced our team to move beyond our initial excitement about the
    solution and think more carefully about the problem we were actually
    trying to address. Our original idea was largely centred around
    creating a customizable suite of games for autistic learners. As we
    worked through the problem statement, we realized that we needed to
    understand why existing learning and recreational experiences may not
    meet the needs of every autistic learner, what meaningful role our
    application could serve, and what we could reasonably claim about
    its impact.
    
    This process led to several discussions about the language we were
    using and the assumptions behind it. In particular, we became more
    conscious of the diversity among autistic individuals. We did not want
    to design around the assumption that there is one ideal difficulty
    level, interaction style, sensory environment, or form of assistance.
    This influenced the way we framed both the problem and our goals.
    Rather than defining success as making one experience that works for
    every learner, we began defining success in terms of providing
    meaningful choices and allowing the experience to be adapted to
    individual needs.
    
    We also think we were successful in separating performance within an
    activity from broader developmental conclusions. Initially, we wanted
    to describe the statistics produced by the system as directly
    measuring a learner's development. Through our research and
    discussions, we recognized that an improvement in game performance
    does not necessarily demonstrate an improvement in the underlying
    ability outside of that game. This distinction ultimately made our
    goals more precise and helped us define a project that we believe
    will be both useful and responsible.
    
    Finally, establishing measurable success criteria helped turn broad
    ideas such as ``accessible,'' ``personalized,'' and ``engaging'' into
    goals that we can eventually evaluate. This was one of the more
    challenging parts of this deliverable, but it gave us a clearer
    understanding of what we would actually need to demonstrate by the
    end of the project rather than relying on subjectivity.

    \item What pain points did you experience during this deliverable, and how
    did you resolve them?
    Our largest challenge was finding the appropriate balance between
    ambition and responsibility. Autism is a broad spectrum, and the
    needs, abilities, preferences, and experiences of autistic individuals
    vary significantly. Early discussions made it easy for the project to
    expand in many directions at once. We considered numerous
    developmental areas, several types of games, different visual
    environments, multiple input methods, extensive caregiver
    customization, detailed analytics, collaborative activities, and
    other features. Individually, many of these ideas seemed valuable,
    but collectively they created a project whose scope would have been
    difficult to implement and, more importantly, difficult to evaluate
    properly.
    
    Another challenge was deciding what we could responsibly claim the
    application accomplishes. Because the project is related to learning
    and development, language such as ``improve,'' ``develop,'' or
    ``assess'' can imply conclusions that our software alone cannot
    establish. We resolved this by distinguishing between providing
    opportunities to practise a skill and demonstrating improvement in
    that skill outside the application.
    
    Defining engagement also presented a challenge. A learner completing
    an activity does not necessarily mean that they enjoyed it, and
    spending more time in an activity does not necessarily indicate
    positive engagement. We therefore chose not to infer enjoyment
    solely from gameplay statistics. Instead, our current goal explicitly
    considers accessible learner feedback, voluntary continuation or
    replay, early stopping, and situations in which feedback cannot be
    obtained. We also decided to distinguish learner feedback from
    caregiver observations rather than treating one as a substitute for
    the other.
    
    Finally, determining measurable goals required us to make decisions
    before every detail of our verification and validation process is
    known. Rather than leaving the goals vague, we established
    preliminary acceptance targets while explicitly documenting their
    limitations. We recognize that these targets may need refinement as
    our verification and validation planning develops, but establishing
    them now gives us some concrete criteria against which we can
    evaluate our progress.
    
    \item How did you and your team adjust the scope of your goals to ensure
    they are suitable for a Capstone project (not overly ambitious but also of
    appropriate complexity for a senior design project)?
    Our initial concept was considerably broader than the project we
    have now defined. We originally envisioned a large collection of
    games that could address many areas of development, support extensive
    caregiver customization, operate through several visual modes,
    accommodate numerous control schemes, provide detailed longitudinal
    analytics, and potentially allow multiple learners to participate
    collaboratively. While this represented our long-term vision for the
    application, we recognized that attempting to implement every aspect
    within our Capstone would likely result in many partially developed
    features rather than a smaller number of well-designed and
    meaningfully evaluated ones.
    
    We narrowed the scope to three activities addressing routine
    sequencing, functional communication, and short-term memory. This
    decision gives us enough variation to investigate whether a common
    platform can support different forms of practice while still keeping
    the number of activities manageable. More importantly, limiting the
    number of core activities gives us time to focus on the aspects we
    believe distinguish the project: individual configuration,
    accessibility, meaningful learner choice, contextualized performance
    information, and caregiver-supported use.
    
    We also reconsidered what ``personalization'' should mean within the
    scope of the project. Rather than attempting to automatically
    determine what is best for a learner, our current goals emphasize
    giving caregivers and educators meaningful configuration options
    while preserving learner choice. This reduces both the technical
    scope and the risk of the software making unsupported assumptions
    about an individual's needs.
    
    The process of narrowing our scope also changed how we thought about
    complexity. Initially, we associated a stronger Capstone project
    with having more features. Through developing this deliverable, we
    came to recognize that complexity can instead come from designing
    a smaller system carefully through supporting different users,
    maintaining accessibility across activities, recording the context
    behind performance, designing useful configuration mechanisms, and
    verifying that the resulting information can actually be interpreted
    correctly. We chose to prioritize depth, validation, and coherence
    over the number of features implemented.
    
    We do expect our scope to continue evolving as we receive feedback
    from our supervisor, intended users, and TA meetings. However, our
    current goals establish a project that we believe is technically
    substantial while still leaving enough time to investigate whether
    the decisions we make actually serve the people for whom the
    application is being designed.
\end{enumerate}
